# Contribution-led Discussion

Apply the writing requirements to arguments, evidence, sentences, and design decisions. Deliver the prose or revision requested by the user; a list of writing principles alone does not complete a drafting task.

## Required reading and scope

- For every task, read [the 30 operational requirements and acceptance criteria](references/requirements-checklist.md). Apply them during revision and check them before delivery.
- For this user's PhD, Void Monument, Chapter 6, the four dimensions, or B01–B08, also read [the thesis argument brief](references/thesis-context.md) and the relevant cards in [the user-supplied participant context](references/participant-context.md). Read the identity map before making participant/space claims. The brief specifies argumentative work; the cards supply concrete context, quotations, artefacts, reported designs, and source limitations.
- For sentence-level revision, language review, or the first complete draft, read [the failure analyses and revision examples](references/revision-examples.md). Apply their diagnostic method; avoid copying their sentences as templates.
- For other research, apply the writing standards using that study's own contribution, evidence, cases, and chapter structure.

## Prepare an evidence and argument record

Read the supplied Findings, Discussion draft, design records, Chapter 2 or literature materials, and actual section labels. Use the context already available. If missing material affects factual judgement, ask specifically for that item and continue work supported by the available evidence.

Prepare the following internal record for the requested text. Normally, do not include this working table in the delivered prose.

| Record | Required specificity |
| --- | --- |
| Established contribution | State the insight this section must explain; retain the full situated design space formulation for this PhD. |
| Participant experience | ID, living/spatial conditions, bodily actions, objects of perception, short exact quotations, and Findings location. |
| Interpretation | Identify the details supporting a relationship; for example, how B06's repeated kneeling, touching, and wiping developed familiarity with surfaces. |
| Comparison | The shared problem, the different relationships in A and B, and the insight gained by considering them together. |
| Theoretical work | The source's precise claim, the identified relationship it explains, and the further design problem this study addresses during translation. |
| My decision | Actions such as I interpreted/selected/kept/arranged, with their specific objects, evidence, and reasons. |
| Visitor encounter | Implemented conditions for seeing, hearing, approaching, or acting; distinguish design intentions from measured responses. |
| Missing evidence | Record missing quotations, design records, source support, or labels individually; never fill these gaps through speculation. |

Distinguish verified primary evidence, user-supplied research context and reported design descriptions, earlier assistant-draft suggestions, and sentences used only to demonstrate writing. Use the supplied context as the current working account; do not repeatedly request facts it already supplies. Check exact wording and manuscript references for final citation. Draft suggestions and examples cannot become implementation claims. Reported features do not establish visitor outcomes. B07 is identified as interview only, without an independent numbered VR space. Case descriptions in this skill do not replace Chapter 5 or actual design records.

## Develop the prose

Begin from the established contribution and explain its significance and scope. Theory positions the contribution, participant material specifies it, comparison produces further insight, and my design practice demonstrates how it operates. These are argumentative responsibilities; paragraph order can follow the material.

For each dimension, readers must be able to locate the following content, which may extend across adjacent paragraphs:

1. The translation problem addressed and its place in the overall contribution.
2. The participant context, actions, quotations, and Findings references needed to understand that problem.
3. An explicit inference from comparison; readers should not have to infer it themselves from two case descriptions.
4. Why the theory appears here, what it explains, and the further step this study addresses.
5. What I interpreted, which design decision I made, and how it changed the conditions of the visitor encounter.
6. Why the components of the dimension's name apply, and what the study adds.

Use these as checks for a complete argument. Do not turn them into four identical six-paragraph sequences. Reuse Findings material for new argumentative work. When compressing it, retain the context, actions, and quotations needed for the inference; remove the already completed discovery process and irrelevant background.

## Sentence and paragraph requirements

Each sentence must do identifiable work: state a precise claim, present evidence, interpret it, compare cases, position theory, explain researcher action, or advance a conclusion. Identify what each sentence takes from its predecessor and what reason it gives for the next. Put otherwise implicit inferential steps into words.

Whenever using relationship, condition, meaning, experience, process, specific, or particular, check that its object is explicit. Name visibility, boundaries, touch, surfaces, sound sources, rooms, routes, or permitted actions whenever the evidence allows. For each this/these, check that the reader can identify one clear referent.

The paragraph opening must identify its analytical task. Begin with a claim, an empirical problem, an unresolved issue from the previous paragraph, or a researcher action as appropriate. Review consecutive openings and the development of all four dimensions for repeated templates. Randomly substituting transitions does not create meaningful variation.

Use I/my for analysis, interpretation, and design decisions in an individual PhD. Retain necessary academic terms and explain them through cases and reasoning. Remove empty academic packaging.

## Three prohibitions

### Defensive writing

Do not pre-empt imagined objections with I do not claim, I am not suggesting, This should not be understood as, This does not mean, Rather than claiming, or Chinese equivalents such as “我并不是要声称” and “这不应被理解为”. State the supported claim directly. Describe genuine limitations through the specific gap, the judgement it affects, and the applicable scope in the appropriate limitations discussion. Preserve necessary uncertainty when removing defensive framing.

### Inflated ambition and contribution claims

Do not portray this study as redefines embodied interaction, transforms participatory design, establishes a new paradigm, proves, or as a universal conclusion covering all VR memory design. Every contribution claim must be grounded in this study's case comparisons, analysis, or concrete design decisions. My study shows can still introduce an exaggerated claim; inspect the whole proposition. Specify what the research actually identifies or addresses.

### Negation followed by a replacement claim

Prohibit “不是……而是……” and equivalent constructions throughout generated text: not X but Y, not merely/simply/just X but Y, not only X but also Y, rather than, instead of, “并非……而是……”, “不仅……更是……”, and “与其……不如……”. Rewrite less X than Y and beyond X to Y when they perform the same corrective opposition. Splitting the construction across sentences, inserting a dash, or beginning the next sentence with Rather/Instead does not remove the problem.

State the substantive claim directly. If both facts have empirical value, describe them separately and explain their relationship. Do not use but/while/whereas to manufacture tension. Factual negation can stand on its own, such as a participant's inability to see a boundary. Preserve exact quotations; select a suitable short excerpt where possible, and never alter quoted speech to satisfy a stylistic rule.

These prohibitions apply to English prose, corresponding Chinese paragraphs, and generated explanatory text. Prohibited phrases listed in these instructions serve only to identify failures.

## Review and delivery

Review the argument first, sentences second, and bilingual alignment and formatting last. Assess every one of the 30 requirements as satisfied, revise, missing evidence, or outside this task's scope. Never mark missing evidence as a pass; briefly identify relevant gaps in the output. Usually keep the internal checklist out of the prose. When the user requests an audit, show the specific issue, original sentence, revision, and reason.

Recheck each case's Findings reference, quotation accuracy, the concrete relationship between theory and contribution, support for every design fact, evaluation evidence for visitor responses, missing cross-sentence inferences, and all three prohibited rhetorical patterns.

For this PhD, continue the established English → Chinese paragraph-aligned delivery. English must function as thesis prose; Chinese must naturally express the same substantive argument. Match claims, cases, quotations, design actions, inferences, scope, and certainty paragraph by paragraph. If Chinese checking reveals missing reasoning, repair the English too. For other tasks, follow the requested language. Follow any explicit subsequent change to the requested output format.

Deliver the full requested scope. Complete a whole section when requested. For a local revision, preserve relevant context and repair affected transitions. A demonstration paragraph cannot substitute for a requested complete delivery.
