# Contribution-led Discussion

Write from the contribution already established by the study. Explain what the findings mean, what comparison reveals, and what the study adds to an existing scholarly conversation. Keep complexity in the argument and clarity in each sentence.

## Establish the scope and evidence

Read the supplied findings, current Discussion, contribution statement, design records, relevant literature, and actual section labels before drafting claims that depend on them. Use the material already available; ask only for missing information that materially affects the argument. Never invent quotations, participant details, implementation decisions, visitor responses, citations, or LaTeX labels. Distinguish an intended design effect from an observed visitor outcome. Flag unresolved evidence or references explicitly.

For the originating immersive-translation PhD, read [the thesis context](references/thesis-context.md). Those cases and four dimensions belong to that project; do not transplant them into another study. For a fidelity review against the user's finalized requirements, read [the requirements checklist](references/requirements-checklist.md).

Identify the requested output and language. Use I/my for the researcher's analysis, interpretation, and design decisions in an individual PhD. Preserve genuine quoted or externally authored uses of we. Do not convert unrelated collaborative work into a single-author study.

## Build the argument from the contribution

Start at the level of the study's contribution. Literature positions, explains, and sharpens it; participant evidence specifies it; cross-case analysis shows what it adds; the researcher's practice demonstrates how it operates. This is an argumentative orientation, not a mandatory sentence or paragraph order.

Reuse Findings material only when it performs new argumentative work. Retain the lived context, bodily actions, spatial conditions, and participant language necessary to understand the claim. Avoid replaying the full route from participant account through analysis and design to the emergence of a dimension.

Make comparisons explicit: identify the shared issue, explain the consequential difference, and state what becomes knowable by examining the accounts together. Two consecutive case summaries do not constitute cross-case analysis. Do not invent a second case if only one is supplied.

Establish the empirical relationship through analysis before asking theory to explain its importance. State the further problem the study addresses and how the evidence or design practice addresses it. Do not use theory to substitute for analysis or present the study merely as confirmation of an author. Add literature only when it clarifies what the contribution adds relative to existing work. Verify new references and their actual claims; a research briefing is a lead, not sufficient evidence for a citation.

Explain dimension names naturally at their first substantive introduction, including why each meaningful component of the name fits the analysis. Each dimension must show what the study adds beyond its definition. Do not inflate connected design concerns into separate grand theories.

## Preserve participant voice and designer accountability

Retain distinctive short quotations or keywords when they carry analytical value, usually 2–8 words. Follow a quotation with concrete interpretation: explain what the person perceived, did, or understood and why that matters. An abstract synonym for the quotation is not analysis. Keep wording and attribution traceable to the source, and resolve competing quote variants before presenting them as exact speech.

Attach a verified Findings section reference to each reused participant example. Use the manuscript's actual labels in final LaTeX, such as `Sec.~\ref{verified-label}`. When a label is unavailable, mark it for verification instead of fabricating one.

Keep the chain of responsibility visible: the participant said, drew, or did something; I interpreted it in a stated way; I made a named design decision; the resulting form made something available to the visitor. Name the change in light, boundary, geometry, material, proximity, sound, movement, viewpoint, interaction, scale, or access. Replace "this informed the design" with the supported decision and its consequence. Do not claim an implementation from an illustrative writing example alone.

## Write complete, connected prose

Use plain, direct language without reducing conceptual precision. Retain useful academic terms and explain them through the argument. Remove academic packaging such as "makes visible the possibility of" when a direct verb states the actual action.

Name what the evidence permits you to name. Words such as particular, specific, certain, relationship, condition, possibility, meaning, experience, difference, interpretation, and process are not banned; they must not conceal known concrete content. If "specific" remains, immediately identify what is specific. Prefer "repeated physical contact and familiarity with the room" to an unexplained "particular body–environment relationship."

Complete the reasoning between sentences. When a sentence introduces a change or problem, explain who acts, what they do, why that follows, and what results. Do not jump from a participant account to a theoretical name and then to a concept without showing the interpretive steps. Use this/these only when the referent is unambiguous.

Give each paragraph a clear opening suited to its role: a contribution, an empirical problem, a researcher action, or a connection with the previous argument. Vary openings because their argumentative roles differ. Avoid repeated templates such as "The question of X became", "X also raised", or "Taken together". Let paragraph length and structure follow the material; do not repeat definition–case A–case B–theory–contribution for every dimension.

State supported claims positively and proportionately. Avoid habitual "not X but Y", "not only", "rather than", "instead of", and tension manufactured with while. Preserve substantive empirical differences and necessary negation. Remove defensive disclaimers such as "I do not claim" or "This should not be understood as"; state genuine limitations directly in the appropriate place. Avoid unsupported claims to redefine a field, establish a paradigm, prove a theory, or generalise to all VR design.

## Close and check

Consolidate the established contribution at the section ending. Develop the next section from the problem already established. Do not introduce a surprise framework, new research questions, or another conceptual layer merely to create a conclusion.

Review each sentence for information, necessity, and logical connection: What does it tell the reader? Why here? What does it take from the previous sentence? What does it establish for the next? Would deletion lose any argument? Remove filler and supply missing inferential steps.

Then check the whole section for contribution-led orientation, necessary participant context, exact short quotations, verified cross-references, analytical comparison, purposeful theory, named researcher actions, concrete design consequences, explained dimension names, appropriate authorship, and proportionate claims. Confirm that evidence was not invented to satisfy the checklist.

When English/Chinese checking is requested, deliver corresponding paragraphs. English is thesis prose; Chinese should make the reasoning readily inspectable through natural language. Match substantive claims, evidence, attribution, agency, and certainty across both versions. Chinese must not add reasoning or evidence missing from English. For continuation of the originating PhD's established bilingual workflow, retain that paired format unless the user changes it. Otherwise follow the requested language without imposing bilingual output.

Deliver the requested prose or revision, with only useful notes on unresolved evidence or substantive changes. Keep internal argument scaffolds out of the thesis prose unless the user asks to see them.
